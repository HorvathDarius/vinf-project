1989 Japanese F1 Grand Prix Race Report & Results

Leopold 22 Oct 1989 11 Jun 5 min read
Share
X Facebook WhatsApp Reddit
←Previous Grand Prix1989 Spanish F1 GPFull season1989 F1 CalendarNext Grand Prix1989 Australian F1 GP→

Event: Japanese Grand Prix
Track: Suzuka Circuit

Weather: overcast, mild, dry

Alessandro Nannini won his 1st F1 World Championship race at the 1989 Japanese F1 GP. The Benetton driver started from the third row and won on the Suzuka circuit for the first time. It was also his 1st win of the season and the 2nd win for the Benetton F1 team.

Suzuka, the war zone

Alain Prost arrived in Japan on 76 points, Ayrton Senna on 60. Under the eleven-best-from-sixteen rule Prost could only add a second place or a victory to his total; Senna, with eight DNFs to drop, could add anything. Mathematically, Senna had to win both remaining races. Mathematically, Prost had to finish second to Senna in at least one of them to clinch the title. The same Suzuka denouement as 1988, with the polarities reversed. Days before practice began Prost issued a public warning to his teammate. If Senna tried to force the move again, as he believed Senna had done at Silverstone and Estoril 1988, Prost would not move over. "This time," he told the French press, "I will not open the door."

Inside McLaren the relationship had broken. Ron Dennis cancelled the customary Marlboro press conference rather than risk an open row. Prost was speaking only to Neil Oatley and Tim Wright. He had received his second new chassis of the year; Senna had received his sixth. He had been told the spare car was now reserved for Senna; the previous race's spare, the MP4/5 development car Pirro had thousands of kilometres on, was now his back-up. Jean-Marie Balestre, with his customary tact, publicly demanded that Honda treat its drivers equally, exactly the intervention guaranteed to enrage Senna's already paranoid sense of grievance.

The paddock also produced two big structural changes. Gérard Larrousse confirmed his alliance with the Japanese Espo group of Kazuo Ito for 1990, 51% of the team capital for Espo, Aguri Suzuki and Éric Bernard signed as drivers, Philippe Alliot departing to Ligier in the spot vacated by Olivier Grouillard. Jackie Oliver sold a controlling stake in Arrows to Wataru Ohashi's Footwork Express Ltd., the team would become Arrows-Footwork in 1990. Mansell, having spent the fortnight at Fiorano setting an unofficial track record, met Balestre to settle the Estoril dispute. He stayed in F1; he intended to win both remaining races.

Senna's pole, Prost's launch

Senna took his 12th pole of the season, and Honda's 50th, in 1m38.041s, a clear 1.7 seconds ahead of Prost, who as usual spent qualifying setting up his race car rather than chasing one-shot times. The Ferraris of Berger and Mansell lined up third and fourth, two seconds back. Patrese was fifth in the rebuilt Williams FW13. Nannini sixth in the Benetton, in part thanks to a new qualifying-only Ford V8 spec. Boutsen seventh. Behind: Alliot a brilliant eighth in the Larrousse, Modena ninth, Larini a startling tenth on Pirelli's softest compound.

The 130,000-strong Sunday crowd, Suzuka's biggest yet, got the start it had paid for. Prost beat Senna away cleanly, the Brazilian fluffing his launch and only just keeping Berger behind. Nannini, jumping past a poor-starting Mansell, ran fourth into the first corner. By lap five Prost led Senna by 3.8s; by lap ten by 4.9s; by lap fourteen by 4.2s, with Berger third at fifteen seconds, Nannini fourth, Mansell now beginning to charge from a long way back. Prost's tyre-warmed pace was outstanding. The world champion-in-waiting was driving the most controlled race of his year.

The chicane collision

The pit-stop sequence between laps nineteen and twenty-five reordered the chasing pack but not the front two. Prost stopped on lap twenty-one for 7.8s; Senna pitted a lap later, and his left-front took ten seconds to fix. Senna emerged five seconds behind Prost. From lap twenty-eight onwards he began to claw the gap closed, 2.3s on lap thirty, 1.5s on lap thirty-one, back to 3.3s as Prost responded on lap thirty-three. Berger's gearbox failed on lap thirty-four to elevate Nannini to third. Mansell's V12 began to smoke on lap forty and he retired four laps later. By lap forty-five Senna was within four-tenths of his teammate, the McLaren in his exhausts. By lap forty-six he had drawn a single conclusion: there was one place to make the pass, the Casio Triangle chicane, and it had to come now.

On lap forty-seven, approaching the chicane, Senna dived inside Prost from a distance that surprised everyone watching, using the pit-entry overflow to gain track width. As the overflow narrowed he was only level with the McLaren. He braked on the concrete; Prost, choosing his apex with the words of his pre-race warning still hanging in the paddock, turned in. The Ferraris of Berger and Mansell had retired; the championship hung on the next two seconds. Senna's left-front clipped Prost's right-side pod. The two McLarens locked together, ploughed onto the chicane's run-off, and rolled to a halt with wheels intertwined. Senna gave Prost a sarcastic round of applause; Prost, stalled and motionless, glared back, climbed out and walked. Race marshals pushed Senna's car to restart it. The McLaren rejoined with its front wing pointed at the sky.

Senna pitted at the end of the next lap for an 18-second new-nose-plus-tyres stop and rejoined ten seconds behind Nannini, who had quietly inherited the lead. The Brazilian's fight back was extraordinary, five seconds taken from Nannini in a single lap, but it was meaningless. Three race-direction findings were already pointing at his disqualification: the use of the pit-entry overflow as a passing lane, the marshal-assisted restart, and the bypassing of the chicane to rejoin. By lap fifty-one Senna had caught Nannini, drafted past him at the chicane on what looked like a comfortable winning move, and led until the flag. Cesare Fiorio's smile in the Ferrari garage said everything: Senna's drive was being recorded only as fastest lap.

Nannini wins, Prost crowned

A Japanese race steward approached Alessandro Nannini in the paddock with cardboard solemnity: "Would you like to climb the podium first?" The Sienese driver, slightly unsteady with disbelief, climbed up between Patrese and Boutsen. The Benetton's first win since Mexico 1986, and the first ever for the Nannini family in Formula 1, was wholly deserved. Nannini had eased his pace before the chicane collision rather than after, ensuring Senna would not have overtaken him on pure pace. "I don't really love victories that come from a disqualification," he said quietly. "But yes, it's still a victory." Patrese took second for Williams-Renault, Boutsen third, ten constructors' points that pushed Williams back ahead of Ferrari for the season's second place. Piquet finished fourth for Lotus, Brundle fifth for Brabham, Warwick sixth for Arrows on a day his car had no business scoring.

Up in Race Control, Senna was being told he had been disqualified. Ron Dennis announced his intention to appeal, an appeal whose direct target was, technically, his own other driver. Prost spun on his heel and left. Senna's defence, delivered in measured tones to a Japanese press corps utterly devoted to him: "I had no other way to overtake. Someone who should not have been there closed the door on me. That's all. With any other driver than Prost, he would have left me room." Prost's: "I had no reason to leave Senna room because I was faster than him, and there wasn't space in that place. I was on my line. He was behind. Taking the run-off was too easy." The appeal would be heard in Paris; the result, eventually, was the one the rule book demanded. Alain Prost had won his third Formula One World Championship, in a Suzuka afternoon that left even his supporters with a faintly metallic taste. One race remained. Australia, in two weeks' time. A McLaren team-mate war that had defined the sport for two seasons was, mercifully, almost over.
